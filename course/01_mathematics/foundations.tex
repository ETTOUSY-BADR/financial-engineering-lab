\chapter{Analysis, Calculus, and Convexity}

\begin{objectives}
Develop the language of sets, limits, continuity, differentiation, and convexity;
derive approximation and optimality results used throughout finance; and learn to
separate a formal calculation from a theorem whose assumptions have been verified.
\end{objectives}

\section{Sets, functions, and proof}

Mathematics starts by fixing the universe in which a claim is made. If $A$ and $B$
are sets, $A\subseteq B$ means every element of $A$ belongs to $B$. The union,
intersection, complement, and Cartesian product are denoted $A\cup B$, $A\cap B$,
$A^c$, and $A\times B$. In probability an outcome lies in a sample space; in
optimization a decision lies in a feasible set; in econometrics an estimand is a
functional of a probability law. Confusing these objects produces errors that no
amount of computation can repair.

\begin{definition}[Function and inverse image]
A function $f:A\to B$ assigns exactly one $f(x)\in B$ to each $x\in A$. For
$C\subseteq B$, its inverse image is
\[
f^{-1}(C)=\{x\in A:f(x)\in C\}.
\]
An inverse function exists on all of $B$ only when $f$ is bijective.
\end{definition}

Inverse images, rather than ordinary inverses, are fundamental in probability:
$X^{-1}((-\infty,x])$ is an event even when a random variable $X$ is not one-to-one.
A proof by contradiction assumes the negation of a statement and derives an
impossibility. A proof by induction establishes a base case and shows that truth at
$n$ implies truth at $n+1$. A counterexample defeats a universal claim with one
admissible case.

\begin{example}[A quantifier matters]
The statement $\E[XY]=\E[X]\E[Y]$ for all integrable $X,Y$ is false. Take $Y=X$
with $\Var(X)>0$. The equality would imply $\E[X^2]=\E[X]^2$, hence zero variance.
The equality does hold under independence. The missing hypothesis is the entire
difference between a useful proposition and a false slogan.
\end{example}

\section{Sequences, limits, and completeness}

\begin{definition}[Convergence]
A sequence $(x_n)$ in $\R$ converges to $x$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is an
$N$ such that $n\geq N$ implies $\abs{x_n-x}<\varepsilon$. A sequence is Cauchy if
for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists $N$ such that $m,n\geq N$ imply
$\abs{x_m-x_n}<\varepsilon$.
\end{definition}

The completeness of $\R$ says that every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges
to a real number. It supports existence arguments, numerical convergence, and the
construction of integrals. A bounded monotone sequence converges. Compactness is the
multidimensional workhorse: in $\R^n$, a set is compact exactly when it is closed and
bounded.

\begin{theorem}[Extreme-value theorem]
If $K\subset\R^n$ is nonempty and compact and $f:K\to\R$ is continuous, then $f$
attains both a minimum and a maximum on $K$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $m=\inf_{x\in K}f(x)$ and choose $x_k\in K$ with $f(x_k)\downarrow m$.
Compactness gives a convergent subsequence $x_{k_j}\to x^\star\in K$. Continuity
implies $f(x_{k_j})\to f(x^\star)$, so $f(x^\star)=m$. Apply the same argument to
$-f$ for the maximum.
\end{proof}

Existence is not uniqueness and uniqueness is not computational stability. A
portfolio objective may attain a minimum while having many minimizers; a unique
minimum may move dramatically under a small change in expected returns.

\section{Continuity and differentiation}

A function $f:\R^n\to\R^m$ is continuous at $x$ when $x_k\to x$ implies
$f(x_k)\to f(x)$. For scalar $f$, differentiability at $x$ means there is a linear
map represented by the gradient $\nabla f(x)$ such that
\[
f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\transpose h+o(\norm{h}).
\]
For a vector-valued function the derivative is its Jacobian. The Hessian
$\nabla^2f(x)$ collects second partial derivatives.

\begin{theorem}[Multivariate Taylor formula]
If $f$ is three times continuously differentiable on a neighborhood of the segment
between $x$ and $x+h$, then
\[
f(x+h)=f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\transpose h+
\frac12h^\transpose\nabla^2f(x)h+R_3,
\qquad \abs{R_3}\leq C\norm{h}^3
\]
for a finite $C$ determined by third derivatives on that segment.
\end{theorem}

Delta--gamma risk is this theorem applied to a derivative value $V(S)$:
\[
\Delta V\approx \Delta^\transpose\Delta S+
\frac12\Delta S^\transpose\Gamma\Delta S.
\]
The approximation is local. Large jumps, barrier crossings, volatility moves, and
time decay can make the omitted terms economically decisive.

\begin{example}[Duration and convexity]
For a bond price $P(y)=\sum_{k=1}^n C_k\e^{-yt_k}$,
\[
\frac{\Delta P}{P}\approx
-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y+\frac12\mathcal C(\Delta y)^2,\quad
D_{\mathrm{mod}}=-\frac{P'(y)}{P(y)},\quad
\mathcal C=\frac{P''(y)}{P(y)}.
\]
The first-order term loses value when yields rise; positive convexity offsets part
of the loss for moves of either sign.
\end{example}

\section{Convex sets and convex functions}

\begin{definition}[Convexity]
A set $C\subseteq\R^n$ is convex if $\theta x+(1-\theta)y\in C$ for all $x,y\in C$
and $\theta\in[0,1]$. A function $f:C\to\R$ is convex when
\[
f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\leq\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y).
\]
It is strictly convex when the inequality is strict for distinct $x,y$ and
$\theta\in(0,1)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[First-order characterization]
Let $C$ be open and convex and let $f:C\to\R$ be differentiable. Then $f$ is convex
if and only if
\[
f(y)\geq f(x)+\nabla f(x)^\transpose(y-x)
\quad\text{for every }x,y\in C.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If $f$ is convex, apply convexity to $x+t(y-x)$, rearrange, divide by $t>0$, and let
$t\downarrow0$. Conversely, define $g(t)=f(x+t(y-x))$. The proposed inequality
applied along the segment makes $g'$ nondecreasing; integrating from $0$ to
$\theta$ and from $\theta$ to $1$ gives the chord inequality.
\end{proof}

If $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, convexity is equivalent to
$\nabla^2f(x)\succeq0$ everywhere. Jensen's inequality is the probabilistic face of
convexity.

\begin{theorem}[Jensen's inequality]
For integrable $X$ and convex $f$ with $f(X)$ integrable,
\[
f(\E[X])\leq\E[f(X)].
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
At an interior point $m=\E[X]$, a convex function has a supporting affine function:
$f(x)\geq f(m)+a(x-m)$. Take expectations. Boundary cases follow by approximation.
\end{proof}

Jensen explains why volatility affects nonlinear payoffs. For the convex call payoff
$f(S_T)=(S_T-K)^+$, dispersion can increase expected payoff even when the mean
terminal price is fixed. It also explains why $\E[\log W]\leq\log\E[W]$: arithmetic
average wealth and long-run geometric growth answer different questions.

\section{Implicit functions and comparative statics}

Suppose $F(x,\theta)=0$ defines a calibrated parameter $x$ as data or hyperparameter
$\theta$ changes. If $F$ is continuously differentiable and $F_x\neq0$, the implicit
function theorem gives
\[
\frac{\dd x}{\dd\theta}=-\frac{F_\theta}{F_x}.
\]
For vector equations, replace division by $F_x^{-1}$. Near singularity, comparative
statics explode: this is a mathematical definition of weak identification or
ill-conditioned calibration.

\chapter{Linear Algebra, Geometry, and Factor Structure}

\begin{objectives}
Use vector spaces, projections, eigenvalues, singular values, and positive
semidefinite matrices to understand portfolios, regressions, factors, and numerical
conditioning.
\end{objectives}

\section{Vector spaces and norms}

A vector space is a set closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication.
The span of columns of $A\in\R^{m\times n}$ is its column space; the null space is
$\{x:Ax=0\}$. The rank--nullity theorem gives
\[
\rank(A)+\dim\ker(A)=n.
\]
Thus a system $Ax=b$ is uniquely solvable for every $b$ only when a square $A$ has
full rank.

A norm satisfies positivity, homogeneity, and the triangle inequality. Common norms
are $\norm{x}_1=\sum_i\abs{x_i}$, $\norm{x}_2=(x^\transpose x)^{1/2}$, and
$\norm{x}_\infty=\max_i\abs{x_i}$. They encode different geometry. An $\ell_1$
constraint encourages sparse exposures; an $\ell_2$ constraint spreads shrinkage;
an $\ell_\infty$ constraint caps each coordinate.

\begin{theorem}[Cauchy--Schwarz]
For an inner-product space,
\[
\abs{\inner{x}{y}}\leq\norm{x}\norm{y}.
\]
Equality holds exactly when $x$ and $y$ are linearly dependent.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For $y\neq0$, nonnegativity of
$\norm{x-t y}^2$ at $t=\inner{x}{y}/\norm{y}^2$ yields
$0\leq\norm{x}^2-\abs{\inner{x}{y}}^2/\norm{y}^2$.
\end{proof}

Cauchy--Schwarz proves the upper bound $\abs{\Corr(X,Y)}\leq1$ and provides the
geometry of the maximum Sharpe ratio.

\section{Orthogonal projection and least squares}

Let $X\in\R^{n\times k}$ have full column rank. The least-squares problem
$\min_b\norm{y-Xb}_2^2$ has normal equations $X^\transpose Xb=X^\transpose y$.
Hence
\[
\widehat b=(X^\transpose X)^{-1}X^\transpose y,\qquad
\widehat y=P_Xy,\quad
P_X=X(X^\transpose X)^{-1}X^\transpose.
\]
The residual $M_Xy=(I-P_X)y$ is orthogonal to every column of $X$.

\begin{proposition}[Frisch--Waugh--Lovell]
Partition $X=[X_1\ X_2]$. The coefficient on $X_2$ in the regression of $y$ on
$X_1,X_2$ equals the regression coefficient of $M_1y$ on $M_1X_2$, where
$M_1=I-P_{X_1}$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Minimize $\norm{y-X_1b_1-X_2b_2}^2$ first over $b_1$ for fixed $b_2$. The minimized
residual is $M_1(y-X_2b_2)$. Differentiating its squared norm in $b_2$ gives the
partialled-out regression.
\end{proof}

The theorem clarifies what a regression coefficient means: it uses variation in a
regressor left after its linear association with controls has been removed. It does
not, by itself, make that residual variation exogenous.

\section{Eigenvalues, covariance, and principal components}

A symmetric matrix $A$ admits an orthogonal eigendecomposition
$A=Q\Lambda Q^\transpose$. It is positive semidefinite when
$x^\transpose Ax\geq0$ for all $x$, equivalently when every eigenvalue is
nonnegative. A covariance matrix is positive semidefinite because
\[
a^\transpose\Sigma a=\Var(a^\transpose X)\geq0.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Spectral theorem]
Every real symmetric $A$ has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors and real
eigenvalues. Consequently $A=Q\Lambda Q^\transpose$ for orthogonal $Q$.
\end{theorem}

For centered data matrix $R$, principal component analysis diagonalizes
$S=R^\transpose R/(T-1)$. The first loading $q_1$ solves
\[
\max_{\norm{q}=1}q^\transpose Sq
\]
and equals the eigenvector associated with the largest eigenvalue.

\begin{proof}
Write $q=Qa$ in the eigenbasis. Then
$q^\transpose Sq=\sum_i\lambda_i a_i^2\leq\lambda_1\sum_i a_i^2=\lambda_1$.
Equality obtains at $q=q_1$.
\end{proof}

In a yield curve, the first three empirical components are often interpreted as
level, slope, and curvature. Those labels are sample-dependent summaries, not
structural shocks. Sign is arbitrary, component order can change, and standard PCA
ignores measurement error and dynamics.

\section{Singular values and conditioning}

Any $A\in\R^{m\times n}$ has a singular-value decomposition
$A=U\Sigma V^\transpose$. The nonzero singular values are square roots of the
positive eigenvalues of $A^\transpose A$. The two-norm condition number is
$\kappa_2(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min}$ for full-rank $A$.

If $\kappa_2(A)$ is large, small perturbations in inputs can produce large changes in
solutions. Expected-return optimization, high-dimensional regression, and implied
parameter inversion are commonly ill-conditioned. Regularization changes the
question to stabilize the answer:
\[
\widehat\beta_{\text{ridge}}
=\argmin_\beta\{\norm{y-X\beta}^2+\lambda\norm{\beta}^2\}
=(X^\transpose X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\transpose y.
\]

\begin{warning}
An invertible sample covariance matrix is not necessarily reliable. When the number
of assets is close to the sample length, its smallest eigenvalues are estimated with
enormous relative error. Optimization amplifies precisely those errors.
\end{warning}

\section{Factor models}

A linear factor representation is
\[
r_t=\alpha+Bf_t+\varepsilon_t,\qquad
\E[\varepsilon_t\mid f_t]=0,\qquad
\Cov(\varepsilon_t,f_t)=0.
\]
It implies
\[
\Sigma_r=B\Sigma_fB^\transpose+\Sigma_\varepsilon.
\]
This low-rank plus idiosyncratic structure reduces estimation dimension and makes
risk attribution possible. It does not decide whether factors are traded, causal,
or merely statistical.

\begin{example}[Marginal contribution to variance]
For portfolio variance $\sigma_p^2=w^\transpose\Sigma w$,
\[
\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w_i}
=\frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p},\qquad
RC_i=w_i\frac{(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p}.
\]
Euler's theorem for homogeneous functions gives $\sum_iRC_i=\sigma_p$.
\end{example}

\chapter{Optimization and Dynamic Decisions}

\begin{objectives}
Derive first- and second-order conditions, understand constraint qualifications and
duality, solve canonical portfolio programs, and formulate sequential decisions by
dynamic programming.
\end{objectives}

\section{Unconstrained and equality-constrained optimization}

At an interior local minimum of differentiable $f$, $\nabla f(x^\star)=0$. If $f$ is
twice differentiable, a positive definite Hessian at a stationary point is
sufficient for a strict local minimum. Convexity upgrades local optimality to global
optimality.

For
\[
\min_x f(x)\quad\text{subject to}\quad h(x)=0,
\]
the Lagrangian is $\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^\transpose h(x)$.
When gradients of active constraints are linearly independent, a local solution
satisfies $\nabla_x\mathcal L=0$ and $h(x)=0$.

\begin{example}[Global minimum-variance portfolio]
For positive definite $\Sigma$,
\[
\min_w\frac12w^\transpose\Sigma w
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\ones^\transpose w=1.
\]
Stationarity gives $\Sigma w-\lambda\ones=0$. Enforcing the constraint yields
\[
w_{\mathrm{GMV}}=
\frac{\Sigma^{-1}\ones}{\ones^\transpose\Sigma^{-1}\ones},
\qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{GMV}}^2=
\frac{1}{\ones^\transpose\Sigma^{-1}\ones}.
\]
\end{example}

For a target mean $m$, define
\[
A=\ones^\transpose\Sigma^{-1}\ones,\quad
B=\ones^\transpose\Sigma^{-1}\mu,\quad
C=\mu^\transpose\Sigma^{-1}\mu,\quad D=AC-B^2.
\]
When $D>0$, the efficient-frontier variance is
$\sigma^2(m)=(Am^2-2Bm+C)/D$.

\section{Inequality constraints and KKT conditions}

Consider convex $f,g_i$ and affine $h$:
\[
\min_x f(x)\quad\text{s.t.}\quad g_i(x)\leq0,\ h(x)=0.
\]
The Karush--Kuhn--Tucker conditions are primal feasibility, dual feasibility
$\lambda_i\geq0$, stationarity, and complementary slackness
$\lambda_i g_i(x)=0$.

\begin{theorem}[KKT sufficiency]
For a convex differentiable program, any primal--dual point satisfying the KKT
conditions is globally optimal. Under Slater's condition, KKT conditions are also
necessary and strong duality holds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Convexity gives
$f(y)\geq f(x^\star)+\nabla f(x^\star)^\transpose(y-x^\star)$.
Substitute stationarity. Convexity of active $g_i$, feasibility, dual feasibility,
and complementary slackness make the remaining expression nonnegative for every
feasible $y$.
\end{proof}

The multiplier is a shadow price: under regularity, it measures the marginal change
in optimized value when a constraint is relaxed. In a risk limit, this quantity is
economically informative only if units and sign conventions are explicit.

\section{Duality and robust optimization}

The Lagrange dual function $q(\lambda,\nu)=\inf_x\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\nu)$ provides
a lower bound on a minimization problem. Maximizing that lower bound is the dual
problem. The primal--dual gap is a numerical certificate when strong duality holds.

Robust optimization replaces a single estimated input with an uncertainty set.
If $\mu\in\{\widehat\mu+u:u^\transpose\Omega^{-1}u\leq\delta^2\}$, then
\[
\min_{\mu}\ w^\transpose\mu
=w^\transpose\widehat\mu-\delta\sqrt{w^\transpose\Omega w}.
\]
Thus ellipsoidal mean uncertainty produces an explicit penalty for sensitivity to
the weakest input.

\section{Utility, certainty equivalents, and Kelly growth}

Expected utility ranks uncertain terminal wealth by $\E[u(W)]$. For increasing,
concave $u$, the Arrow--Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion is
$A(W)=-u''(W)/u'(W)$ and relative risk aversion is $R(W)=WA(W)$.
Exponential utility has constant absolute risk aversion; power utility has constant
relative risk aversion.

For small zero-mean risk $\widetilde X$ added to wealth $W$, a second-order expansion
gives the risk premium
\[
\pi\approx\frac12A(W)\Var(\widetilde X).
\]
With normally distributed return and quadratic or exponential utility, maximizing a
certainty equivalent leads to mean--variance form.

Log utility maximizes expected logarithmic growth. In a one-period approximation
with risky excess return mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, the growth-optimal
fraction is $f^\star\approx\mu/\sigma^2$. Estimation error, fat tails, leverage
constraints, and drawdown preferences make fractional Kelly policies more robust in
practice.

\section{Dynamic programming}

A controlled Markov state evolves as
$x_{t+1}=g(x_t,a_t,\varepsilon_{t+1})$. With reward $r(x,a)$ and discount $\beta$,
the value function satisfies the Bellman equation
\[
V_t(x)=\sup_{a\in\mathcal A(x)}
\left\{r_t(x,a)+\beta\E[V_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\mid x_t=x,a_t=a]\right\}.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Principle of optimality]
If a policy is optimal from an initial state, its continuation after any reachable
state is optimal for the remaining subproblem.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
If a continuation were not optimal, replace it by a better continuation while
keeping earlier actions fixed. This would improve the initial policy, a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

Transaction costs make portfolio choice genuinely dynamic because trading today
changes tomorrow's state. With proportional costs, an inaction region often
emerges: rebalance only when the benefit exceeds the cost of moving toward the
frictionless target.

\chapter{Probability, Information, and Statistical Limits}

\begin{objectives}
Construct probability models with sigma-algebras, work with conditional expectation
and changes of variables, understand modes of convergence, and derive the laws that
justify estimation.
\end{objectives}

\section{Probability spaces and random variables}

\begin{definition}[Probability space]
A probability space is $(\Omega,\F,\Prob)$, where $\Omega$ is the sample space,
$\F$ is a sigma-algebra, and $\Prob:\F\to[0,1]$ is countably additive with
$\Prob(\Omega)=1$.
\end{definition}

A sigma-algebra contains $\Omega$, complements, and countable unions. This closure
ensures that limits of observable events remain observable. A random variable
$X:(\Omega,\F)\to(\R,\B(\R))$ is measurable: $X^{-1}(B)\in\F$ for every Borel set
$B$. Its distribution is the pushforward measure
$\Prob_X(B)=\Prob(X\in B)$.

For a nonnegative random variable, expectation is defined by approximation with
simple functions; for general integrable $X$, write $X=X^+-X^-$. This construction
explains why expectation is linear and why limit-exchange theorems need hypotheses.

\begin{theorem}[Monotone convergence]
If $0\leq X_n\uparrow X$ almost surely, then $\E[X_n]\uparrow\E[X]$, possibly to
infinity.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Dominated convergence]
If $X_n\to X$ almost surely and $\abs{X_n}\leq Y$ for an integrable $Y$, then
$X$ is integrable and $\E[X_n]\to\E[X]$.
\end{theorem}

These theorems justify differentiation or limiting operations under an expectation.
Without domination or uniform integrability, pointwise convergence need not preserve
prices.

\section{Distributions and transforms}

The distribution function $F_X(x)=\Prob(X\leq x)$ is nondecreasing, right-continuous,
and has limits $0$ and $1$. If it is absolutely continuous, its derivative is a
density and
$\E[g(X)]=\int g(x)f_X(x)\dd x$.

The moment-generating function $M_X(t)=\E[\e^{tX}]$, when finite near zero,
determines all moments and the distribution. The characteristic function
$\varphi_X(u)=\E[\e^{iuX}]$ always exists and uniquely determines the law. Fourier
pricing methods exploit
\[
\E[g(X)]=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\R}\widehat g(-u)\varphi_X(u)\dd u
\]
when the transform conditions hold.

Key families include Bernoulli and binomial counts; Poisson arrivals; exponential
waiting times; Gaussian sums; lognormal positive prices; Student distributions with
heavy tails; and generalized extreme-value laws for maxima. A named distribution is
a hypothesis, not a property of a ticker.

\begin{example}[Lognormal moments]
If $X\sim N(m,s^2)$ and $S=\e^X$, then
\[
\E[S^k]=\e^{km+\frac12k^2s^2}.
\]
Thus $\E[S]=\e^{m+s^2/2}$ and
$\Var(S)=\e^{2m+s^2}(\e^{s^2}-1)$. The median $\e^m$ is below the mean.
\end{example}

\section{Conditional expectation}

\begin{definition}[Conditional expectation]
For integrable $X$ and sub-sigma-algebra $\mathcal G\subseteq\F$,
$\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ is the almost-surely unique $\mathcal G$-measurable random
variable such that
\[
\E[\ind_A\E[X\mid\mathcal G]]
=\E[\ind_AX]\quad\text{for all }A\in\mathcal G.
\]
\end{definition}

It is a projection of $X$ onto available information. It is linear, monotone, and
respects known quantities:
$\E[ZX\mid\mathcal G]=Z\E[X\mid\mathcal G]$ when $Z$ is bounded and
$\mathcal G$-measurable.

\begin{theorem}[Tower property]
If $\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$, then
\[
\E[\E[X\mid\mathcal G]\mid\mathcal H]=\E[X\mid\mathcal H].
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
The left side is $\mathcal H$-measurable. For $A\in\mathcal H\subseteq\mathcal G$,
apply the defining integral identity twice. Uniqueness completes the proof.
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[Conditional Jensen]
For convex $\phi$ and suitable integrability,
\[
\phi(\E[X\mid\mathcal G])\leq\E[\phi(X)\mid\mathcal G].
\]
\end{proposition}

\section{Dependence}

Independence is stronger than zero covariance. Copulas separate marginal
distributions from dependence: Sklar's theorem represents a joint distribution as
\[
F(x_1,\ldots,x_d)=C(F_1(x_1),\ldots,F_d(x_d)).
\]
Gaussian copulas have zero asymptotic tail dependence unless correlation is perfect;
Student copulas can generate symmetric tail dependence. Rank correlations are
invariant under strictly monotone marginal transformations but still do not fully
describe joint tails.

\begin{definition}[Tail dependence]
For continuous $X,Y$, upper-tail dependence is
\[
\lambda_U=\lim_{u\uparrow1}
\Prob\!\left(Y>F_Y^{-1}(u)\mid X>F_X^{-1}(u)\right),
\]
when the limit exists. Lower-tail dependence is defined analogously.
\end{definition}

\section{Convergence and limit theorems}

Almost-sure convergence implies convergence in probability, which implies
convergence in distribution. Convergence in $L^p$ is convergence of
$\E[\abs{X_n-X}^p]$ to zero. Reverse implications require additional conditions.

\begin{theorem}[Weak law of large numbers]
If $X_1,X_2,\ldots$ are iid with finite mean $\mu$, then
$\overline X_n\to\mu$ in probability.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
When variance is finite, Chebyshev's inequality yields
\[
\Prob(\abs{\overline X_n-\mu}>\varepsilon)
\leq\frac{\Var(X_1)}{n\varepsilon^2}\to0.
\]
The finite-mean result follows by truncation.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Lindeberg--Lévy central limit theorem]
For iid variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $0<\sigma^2<\infty$,
\[
\sqrt n\,\frac{\overline X_n-\mu}{\sigma}\Rightarrow N(0,1).
\]
\end{theorem}

Financial returns are neither exactly iid nor always finite-variance in useful
samples. Mixing, martingale-difference, and stable-law limit theories address
different departures. Standard errors must match the dependence structure.

\begin{proposition}[Markov and Chebyshev inequalities]
For nonnegative $X$ and $a>0$, $\Prob(X\geq a)\leq\E[X]/a$. Hence for finite-variance
$Y$, $\Prob(\abs{Y-\E Y}\geq a)\leq\Var(Y)/a^2$.
\end{proposition}

\section{Bayesian updating}

With prior $\pi(\theta)$ and likelihood $p(y\mid\theta)$,
\[
\pi(\theta\mid y)
=\frac{p(y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta)}
{\int p(y\mid u)\pi(u)\dd u}.
\]
Posterior prediction integrates parameter uncertainty:
$p(\widetilde y\mid y)=\int p(\widetilde y\mid\theta)\pi(\theta\mid y)\dd\theta$.
This differs from plugging in a point estimate.

\begin{example}[Normal--normal learning]
If $Y_i\mid\mu\sim N(\mu,\sigma^2)$ and
$\mu\sim N(m_0,s_0^2)$ with known $\sigma^2$, then the posterior is normal with
\[
s_n^{-2}=s_0^{-2}+n\sigma^{-2},\qquad
m_n=s_n^2(s_0^{-2}m_0+n\sigma^{-2}\overline Y).
\]
The posterior mean is precision-weighted shrinkage toward the prior.
\end{example}

\chapter{Stochastic Processes and Continuous-Time Finance}

\begin{objectives}
Work with filtrations, martingales, Brownian motion, stochastic integrals, Itô's
formula, stochastic differential equations, and equivalent changes of measure.
\end{objectives}

\section{Time, information, and martingales}

A stochastic process is a family $(X_t)_{t\in T}$ of random variables on a common
probability space. A filtration $(\F_t)$ is increasing: $\F_s\subseteq\F_t$ for
$s\leq t$. A process is adapted if $X_t$ is $\F_t$-measurable. Predictability is a
slightly stronger pre-trade measurability condition required of trading integrands.

\begin{definition}[Martingale]
An adapted integrable process $M$ is a martingale when
\[
\E[M_t\mid\F_s]=M_s,\qquad s\leq t.
\]
Replacing equality by $\leq$ gives a supermartingale and by $\geq$ a submartingale.
\end{definition}

Martingale does not mean independent increments, constant paths, or absence of risk.
It means that conditional on the specified information set, the next expected
change is zero. A discounted traded price is a martingale only under a suitable
pricing measure and market assumptions.

\begin{theorem}[Optional stopping, bounded version]
If $M$ is a martingale and $\tau$ is a bounded stopping time, then
$\E[M_\tau]=\E[M_0]$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For integer time with $\tau\leq N$,
$M_\tau=M_0+\sum_{t=1}^N(M_t-M_{t-1})\ind_{\{\tau\geq t\}}$.
The indicator is $\F_{t-1}$-measurable, so every increment has zero expectation.
Continuous time follows by approximation under the stated regularity.
\end{proof}

Unbounded stopping rules require uniform integrability or related hypotheses.
Doubling strategies appear to beat fair games precisely by violating admissibility
or optional-stopping conditions.

\section{Brownian motion and quadratic variation}

\begin{definition}[Brownian motion]
A process $W$ is standard Brownian motion if $W_0=0$, paths are continuous,
increments are independent and stationary, and
$W_t-W_s\sim N(0,t-s)$ for $t>s$.
\end{definition}

Brownian paths are almost surely nowhere classically differentiable and have infinite
total variation on every interval. Yet their quadratic variation is finite:
\[
\sum_k(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k})^2\to t
\]
in probability as mesh tends to zero. In differential notation,
$(\dd W_t)^2=\dd t$, $\dd W_t\,\dd t=0$, and $(\dd t)^2=0$. These are mnemonic
rules for quadratic covariation, not ordinary infinitesimals.

\section{The Itô integral}

For a simple predictable process
$H_t=\sum_kH_k\ind_{(t_k,t_{k+1}]}(t)$, define
\[
\int_0^T H_t\dd W_t
=\sum_kH_k(W_{t_{k+1}}-W_{t_k}).
\]
The construction extends by completion to square-integrable predictable processes.

\begin{theorem}[Itô isometry]
If $\E\int_0^T H_t^2\dd t<\infty$, then
\[
\E\left[\left(\int_0^TH_t\dd W_t\right)^2\right]
=\E\int_0^TH_t^2\dd t.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
For a simple process, expand the squared sum. Cross terms vanish because later
Brownian increments have conditional mean zero, and diagonal terms equal
$\E[H_k^2](t_{k+1}-t_k)$. Pass to the $L^2$ limit.
\end{proof}

The stochastic integral is a martingale under square integrability. It is defined
with left-endpoint information, encoding that a strategy cannot see the increment it
is about to trade.

\section{Itô's formula}

\begin{theorem}[Itô's formula]
If
$\dd X_t=b(t,X_t)\dd t+a(t,X_t)\dd W_t$ and
$f\in C^{1,2}$, then
\[
\dd f(t,X_t)=
\left(f_t+bf_x+\frac12a^2f_{xx}\right)\dd t
+af_x\dd W_t.
\]
For $d$ dimensions, the second-order term is
$\frac12\tr(aa^\transpose\nabla^2f)\dd t$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof idea]
Apply a second-order Taylor expansion over a fine partition. Terms in
$\Delta t^2$ and $\Delta t\Delta W$ vanish in aggregate; the sum of
$(\Delta W)^2$ converges to elapsed time. Control of the Taylor remainder completes
the limit.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}[Geometric Brownian motion]
If $\dd S_t=\mu S_t\dd t+\sigma S_t\dd W_t$, Itô's formula for $\log S$ gives
\[
\dd\log S_t=(\mu-\tfrac12\sigma^2)\dd t+\sigma\dd W_t.
\]
Hence
$S_t=S_0\exp((\mu-\sigma^2/2)t+\sigma W_t)$, which is positive and satisfies
$\E[S_t]=S_0\e^{\mu t}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Ornstein--Uhlenbeck process]
For $\dd X_t=\kappa(\theta-X_t)\dd t+\sigma\dd W_t$,
\[
X_t=\theta+(X_0-\theta)\e^{-\kappa t}
+\sigma\int_0^t\e^{-\kappa(t-s)}\dd W_s.
\]
It is Gaussian with conditional mean
$\theta+(X_s-\theta)\e^{-\kappa(t-s)}$ and variance
$\sigma^2(1-\e^{-2\kappa(t-s)})/(2\kappa)$.
\end{example}

\section{Existence, generators, and Feynman--Kac}

Global Lipschitz and linear-growth conditions on $b$ and $a$ are sufficient for a
unique strong SDE solution. Many finance diffusions, including square-root models,
need more refined results at boundaries.

The infinitesimal generator is
\[
\mathcal Lf=b^\transpose\nabla f+
\frac12\tr(aa^\transpose\nabla^2f).
\]
If
\[
u(t,x)=\E_{t,x}\left[
\e^{-\int_t^Tc(X_s)\dd s}g(X_T)
+\int_t^T\e^{-\int_t^sc(X_v)\dd v}q(s,X_s)\dd s
\right],
\]
then under regularity $u$ solves
$u_t+\mathcal Lu-cu+q=0$ with $u(T,x)=g(x)$. This Feynman--Kac bridge connects
conditional expectations, pricing, and partial differential equations.

\section{Change of measure}

Let $\theta$ be adapted and satisfy an integrability condition such as Novikov's:
$\E[\exp(\frac12\int_0^T\theta_t^2\dd t)]<\infty$. Define
\[
Z_T=\exp\left(
-\int_0^T\theta_t\dd W_t-\frac12\int_0^T\theta_t^2\dd t
\right),\qquad
\frac{\dd\Q}{\dd\Prob}=Z_T.
\]

\begin{theorem}[Girsanov]
Under $\Q$, the process
$W_t^\Q=W_t+\int_0^t\theta_s\dd s$ is Brownian motion.
\end{theorem}

If a stock obeys $\dd S/S=\mu\dd t+\sigma\dd W^\Prob$ and the money-market rate is
$r$, choose $\theta=(\mu-r)/\sigma$. Then under $\Q$,
$\dd S/S=r\dd t+\sigma\dd W^\Q$. The physical expected return disappears from the
replication price, while volatility remains.

\begin{warning}
An equivalent pricing measure is not a forecast distribution. Risk-neutral
probabilities incorporate risk prices and are designed to price traded payoffs.
Scenario probabilities for risk management belong under a physical or stressed
measure.
\end{warning}

\section{Jumps and semimartingales}

Brownian paths cannot produce discontinuous prices. A jump diffusion may be written
\[
\frac{\dd S_t}{S_{t-}}
=(\mu-\lambda\kappa_J)\dd t+\sigma\dd W_t+(J-1)\dd N_t,
\]
where $N$ is Poisson with intensity $\lambda$ and
$\kappa_J=\E[J-1]$. Jumps alter Itô's formula by adding the exact discontinuity
$f(S_t)-f(S_{t-})$ rather than a local quadratic approximation.

The general no-arbitrage language uses semimartingales because they support a broad
class of stochastic integrals while ruling out pathwise schemes that manufacture
profit from infinite variation.
